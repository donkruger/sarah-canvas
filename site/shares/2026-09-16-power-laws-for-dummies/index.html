Power Laws, Explained for Dummies

Most things you can measure cluster around an average — heights, test scores, commute times. Power laws describe the things that don't: a tiny handful are enormous, and everything else trails off fast, forever.

"A small share of the pea pods in his garden produced most of the peas."

— the observation that started it: Vilfredo Pareto, 1896

Pareto noticed the same lopsided split in land ownership, income, even his peas: roughly 20% of causes producing roughly 80% of the effect. That's the everyday name for a power law — the 80/20 rule — but the real definition is about shape, not the exact numbers.

Same data, two views. Squashed and stretched on normal axes (left); once both axes become logarithmic, that curve straightens out (right) — that straight line is the signature mathematicians look for.

The 80/20 shortcut

You don't need logarithms to use this. If a list is power-law shaped, a small top slice — often around 20% — accounts for most of the total, often around 80%. Not exactly 80/20 every time, but close enough to be a useful gut check: check the top few before assuming the rest matter equally.

Where this shows up

  • Word frequency"The" and "of" appear constantly; most words in the dictionary appear almost never. (Zipf's law)
  • City sizesA few megacities, then a rapid drop to thousands of small towns.
  • WealthPareto's original example — still holds, in most countries, over a century later.
  • EarthquakesSmall tremors happen constantly; massive ones are rare but not "impossible."
  • App downloads / follower countsA handful of apps or accounts dwarf everything else in the store or platform.
  • Customer revenueA small number of accounts often generate most of the revenue — and most of the support tickets too.

Why it matters

  • 1No "typical" size. With a bell curve, the average is a sensible summary. With a power law, the average can be misleading — most items are far below it, and a few blow way past it.
  • 2Outliers aren't glitches. A power law predicts extreme values as a normal part of the pattern, not something to explain away.
  • 3The straight-line test. Plot rank against value on log–log axes. Roughly straight for a good stretch? You're likely looking at a power law.
  • 4Plan for the head, not just the average. If one input can be 100x the typical one, capacity, pricing, and support plans built around "the average case" will break.
Close to home: ticket volume, feature usage, and revenue-per-customer tend to be power-law shaped too — a handful of accounts or issues driving most of the load. Worth a second look next time an average number looks reassuring.
